How it works

Every genome is the complete set of weights and biases of one network — 6 636 numbers. They are never touched by a gradient. The only things that change them are crossover and Gaussian mutation, and the only thing that decides which genomes get copied is whether they won their games.

The three tabs

Train runs the evolution and shows whichever genome is currently leading its generation. Watch Champion sets the best brain going on its own against an opponent you choose — any rung of the ladder, a mirror match against itself, or another evolved brain — at a pace you set, with both decision heads painted live on the board. Play vs Champion puts you on the other side of the same brain.

Both of those tabs let you pick which brain. Mikkel's AI is the one bundled with the page: evolved offline to generation 439, and the strongest that exists here, so it is what you get by default. Once your own training has produced a champion it appears in the list too, alongside the current generation's leader and anything still in your archive. Watching Mikkel's AI fight the material bot while your own population trains in the background is the quickest way to see how far a fresh run still has to go.

Two heads, one decision

The network is deliberately split the way a player thinks. A FROM head gives every square a score for "I want to move this piece", and a TO head gives every square a score for "…and put it there". A move is worth FROM[origin] + TO[origin][destination], and the brain plays the highest-scoring legal move. The two heads are not independent: the TO head's ranking of destinations is conditioned on the origin through a learned query/key product, so a rook and a knight standing on the same square get different maps. Tick show the two heads to watch both distributions live on the board — blue is the piece it wants to move, orange is where it wants it to go. A fourth head picks the promotion piece.

It remembers the last five positions

Each of the 64 squares is described by 30 numbers, and five of them are the whole board, five plies into the past — one complete previous position per channel. Not a summary, the actual placement. So the brain can see what just moved, whether it is shuffling in a repetition, and which way material has been trending. Six more channels are the piece standing there now, two are how many enemy and friendly pieces attack the square, and the rest cover mobility, the last moves played, and where the square sits on the board. On top of that come 36 whole-position numbers: material, castling rights, checks, king pressure, hanging material, the 50-move clock, and the material balance at each of the five remembered positions.

The board is always shown to the network from the mover's own side, so one genome plays white and black equally well.

Why the network is shaped like this

A dense first layer over a chess board would be roughly a hundred thousand weights — far too many for a genetic algorithm to search. Instead one small encoder is shared by all 64 squares and applied to each in turn, next to a learned 64×22 piece-square table that lets a shared encoder still say "this particular square is special". Every one of the 6 636 genes therefore gets feedback from all 64 squares on every single ply.

No search, and what that costs

There is no tree, no lookahead, no evaluation of hypothetical positions. The one exception is a move that delivers checkmate immediately, which is always played — a player with no search has no way to tell a mate from a merely aggressive check. Because it cannot look ahead, everything tactical has to be visible in the input, which is what the attack-count channels are for: a square that is attacked twice and defended once is visibly poisoned, so not hanging pieces is something evolution can actually discover.

How selection works

Each generation every genome plays eight games. Four are self-play against other members of the population (a random pairing and a rank-adjacent pairing, colours swapped in each). Four are against a fixed opponent ladder: a random mover, a capture grabber, a material bot that avoids leaving pieces hanging, and champions pulled from the archive of past winners. Rungs unlock as the population outgrows them, and beating a hard opponent is worth more than beating an easy one.

Every game starts from a different position — a book line or a short random prefix — and each colour-swapped rematch replays the same opening, so a pair of games is a fair comparison rather than two samples of the same game.

The next generation is elites, the reigning champion, champion variants, row-wise crossover children, mutants, whole-row rewrites, and fresh random immigrants. Mutation pressure rises automatically when progress stalls, and genomes that collapse onto an elite get shaken hard so the population never becomes a room full of clones. A new best-of-generation only takes the crown after beating the sitting champion head to head; otherwise the title just tracks luck in an eight-game sample.

Honest expectations

This will not become a strong engine. A searchless policy trained by a genetic algorithm reaches roughly "a beginner who has stopped hanging pieces". The brain shipped with this page was evolved to generation 439 offline; measured afterwards on positions it never trained on it scores 100% against a random mover, 95% against the capture grabber, 50% against the careless bot and 20% against the material bot, and beats untrained genomes of the same architecture 93% of the time.

The percentages are the least interesting part. An untrained genome hangs a piece for nothing on about 6% of its moves; the evolved one does it on about 2%. Learning not to donate material — from the position alone, with no search to discover it — is the whole of what happened here.

What it never learned: it does not castle. The move is generated, legal and scored; it just ranks around 18th of 33 in positions where it is clearly right. The FROM head learned "moving the king is bad", which is usually true and here is fatal, because castling then has to overcome that prior through the TO head alone. It is a genuine cost of splitting the decision in two — and a good example of something obvious in the behaviour and invisible in the score.
