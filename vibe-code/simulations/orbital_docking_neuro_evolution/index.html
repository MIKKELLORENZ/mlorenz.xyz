Learning to Dock

A 6.3 tonne capsule and a space station, both in a real 413 km orbit. The capsule has sixteen thrusters, a laser that only works inside 250 m, and no idea that orbits are counter-intuitive — that burning toward the station makes you miss it, and that most of a rendezvous is spent deliberately doing nothing. Nothing is scripted and there is no gradient descent: brains are mutated, raced against each other on identical scenarios, and bred.

This will not produce a docking. One browser thread at a population of twenty-four is three orders of magnitude short of what the champion took — the headless trainer runs hundreds of brains across many cores for hours. What it is for is watching the machinery: scenarios being auditioned, the field being cut in half between scenarios, and the advantage figure moving. 0 is no better than coasting, 1.0 is as good as the scripted autopilot.

Capture envelope

All six have to hold at the instant the probe crosses the port plane. Arriving beautifully aligned 30% too fast is not a docking, it is damage to two vehicles.

What you are looking at

The view is the LVLH frame, station-centred: V-bar is along the direction of travel, R-bar points down at the Earth, H-bar is out of the orbit plane. The blue track is the path flown; the faint one is the whole flight. The green cone is the approach corridor, the red lines are laser returns off the five retroreflectors on the docking ring — the only thing the network can see well enough to dock with.

The Sun is fixed in inertial space, so it swings all the way round the sky once every 93 minutes and the station spends part of every lap in the Earth's shadow.
