# Flying in Four Dimensions

A drone with eight rotors, ten degrees of freedom and four directions to get
lost in, learning to reach waypoints whose coordinates have four numbers — by
mutation and selection, with no gradient descent anywhere.

This is not a 3D simulation with a spare variable bolted on, and it is not a
slice. It is a rigid body in ℝ⁴: orientation lives in **SO(4)**, angular
velocity is a **bivector** with six components, and the inertia tensor maps
bivectors to bivectors. Being in the right place in *x, y, z* counts for
nothing if you are in the wrong place in *w*.

**[Open the viewer](index.html)** · `node test_math.js` runs the verification
suite · `node train.js` trains from scratch.

## Result

The evolved network flies. 1,604 generations, population 110. On **held-out
missions it has never seen** — four independent seed banks, in a seed range
disjoint from every training seed by construction — the shipped brain
(generation 1604, sha1 `10cc674d3599`) reaches:

| | evolved | hand-written PD pilot |
|---|---|---|
| waypoints / episode | **8.13** | 18.31 |
| worst of four banks | 6.75 | 17.75 |
| mean airtime | 69.7 s | 118.6 s |
| took off | 97% | 100% |

**44% of a competent classical controller**, from mutation and selection alone,
with the network commanding eight rotors directly and nothing resembling an
attitude loop underneath it.

It is inconsistent, and that is worth stating plainly: per-episode results
inside one bank run `[5 17 4 1 14 5 13 8]`. Over 60 fresh missions with the
episode caps lifted, the median is 6 waypoints and the best is **26 waypoints
across 197 seconds of continuous flight** without a crash. It can fly very well;
it cannot yet do so reliably.

### How it got there

The interesting part is the shape of the run. For 435 generations it sat at
**0.00 waypoints on fresh missions** while scoring perfectly respectably on its
own training bank — it had memorised six directions instead of learning to read
the waypoint sensor at all (see the mission-bank section below). Once the bank
was made to rotate:

| | gen 511 | gen 1200 | gen 1604 |
|---|---|---|---|
| waypoints/episode, held out | 0.00 | 6.29 | **8.13** |
| airtime | 5.2 s | 66.8 s | 69.7 s |
| crash rate | 100% | 42% | 53% |

The brain that shipped is **not** the one the training log called the champion.
`pick_champion.js` re-ranked the final population on held-out banks and put
`pop#5` (8.13) above the logged champion (7.88) — which is the entire reason
that step exists.

## Why 4D breaks the usual quadrotor code

Almost every piece of 3D rigid-body intuition is a coincidence of *n* = 3, and
each one has to be replaced rather than generalised:

| 3D | why it fails in 4D | what replaces it |
|---|---|---|
| orientation is a unit quaternion | unit quaternions double-cover SO(3), a different group | a 4×4 matrix in SO(4) |
| you rotate *about an axis* | the orthogonal complement of a plane is a line only when *n* = 3 | you rotate *in a plane*; there are C(4,2) = 6 of them |
| ω and τ are vectors; ω × Iω | there is no cross product in 4D | antisymmetric matrices (bivectors); the commutator [Ω, L] |
| inertia is 3 numbers on the diagonal | — | 6 plane moments, but generated by only 4 |
| yaw is the one harmless rotation | — | *three* harmless rotations |

### Orientation and the equations of motion

`Ω` is defined by v̇ = Ω·v, so a rotation in plane (a, b) at rate ω has
Ω[b][a] = +ω. Angular momentum and torque are

    L = Σ m (v xᵀ − x vᵀ) = J·Ω + Ω·J        τ = Σ (F xᵀ − x Fᵀ)

and Euler's equation in the body frame — derived by differentiating
L_space = R·L·Rᵀ under Ṙ = R·Ω, not asserted — is

    L̇ = τ − [Ω, L]

The commutator is doing exactly the job ω × (Iω) does in 3D, with three extra
planes to couple. Note the sign convention: the commoner `x ∧ v` ordering gives
L = −(JΩ + ΩJ), a sign flip against Ω. Both are correct; mixing them is not,
and the mixture is invisible until the drone rotates the wrong way under torque.

### Inertia is four numbers, not six

The six planes have six moments of inertia, but they are **not free**. They come
from the mass quadrupole J = Σ m x xᵀ as I₍ₐ,ᵦ₎ = Jₐ + Jᵦ, so choosing six
numbers independently would describe no physical body. Here

    J = diag(0.015, 0.003, 0.015, 0.015) kg·m²   over (right, up, forward, ana)

which reproduces the 3D reference quadrotor this project was built from
*exactly*: 0.018 kg·m² in each of the three **tilt** planes (those containing
the thrust axis) and 0.030 kg·m² in each of the three **heading** planes. Mass
spread through the horizontal 3-space and thin along the thrust axis — a
pancake, one dimension up.

The tilt/heading split is the 4D generalisation of "pitch and roll will kill
you, yaw will not": rotations in the three planes containing the thrust axis
tip the drone over, and rotations in the three planes orthogonal to it leave the
thrust direction exactly where it was.

### Eight rotors, at the corners of a cube

A rotor is a scalar actuator: thrust along the airframe's thrust axis, plus a
reaction torque in its own spin plane. Put eight of them at the vertices of a
cube in the 3-space orthogonal to the thrust axis,

    p(s) = (ARM/√3)·( s_R·ê_R + s_F·ê_F + s_A·ê_A ),   s ∈ {−1,+1}³

give rotor *s* the spin plane orthogonal to its own arm (the Hodge dual of p(s)
inside that 3-space — a genuine single rotation plane, perpendicular to the
thrust axis as a real propeller disc must be), with spin direction
σ(s) = s_R·s_F·s_A. Then the map from the eight thrusts to (total thrust, 3 tilt
torques, 3 heading torques) has rows

    1,  s_R,  s_F,  s_A,  s_F·s_A,  s_R·s_A,  s_R·s_F

— seven of the eight **Walsh functions** on {−1,+1}³. Walsh functions are
mutually orthogonal, so the 7×8 control matrix has rank 7 with condition number
exactly 1: all seven quantities are independently commandable and none of them
is a weak direction. The one unreachable combination is the eighth Walsh
function, the checkerboard s_R·s_F·s_A, which produces no net force and no net
torque at all.

That is the reason for the cube, not a coincidence noticed afterwards. Six
rotors on the ±axes (an octahedron) lose rank: each tilt torque becomes slaved
to a heading torque, so the drone cannot lean without spinning. Seven controls
for a 10-DOF body is precisely the 3D quadrotor's bargain — four controls for a
6-DOF body — carried up a dimension.

Because the seven rows are orthogonal, the control allocation has a closed form
(divide each projection by 8) rather than needing a matrix decomposition. The
network does not use it: it commands the eight rotors directly and has to
discover the structure for itself. The closed form exists so the claim can be
tested, and so a hand-written pilot can prove the environment is flyable.

## Does the math check out?

`node test_math.js` — **65 assertions**, all passing. The suite exists because
in 4D almost nothing can be checked by eye: a simulation that has quietly
reduced to "3D with a spare coordinate", or that has one sign wrong in the
bivector algebra, renders exactly like a correct one.

Selected results:

```
A. orientation lives in SO(4)
   cayley(A) orthogonal for random antisymmetric A       max ‖RᵀR − I‖∞ = 6.7e-16
   R stays in SO(4) over a 150 s episode (18,000 steps)  det = 1.000000000000000
   Ω in plane (a,b) rotates e_a toward e_b               matches cos/sin to 1e-5

C. the inertia tensor is a real mass distribution
   I₍ₐ,ᵦ₎ = Jₐ + Jᵦ for all six planes                  ✓
   six plane moments generated by four mass numbers      I₀+I₅ = I₁+I₄ = I₂+I₃

D. eight rotors, seven controllable degrees of freedom
   the 7 control rows are mutually ORTHOGONAL (Walsh)    max |cos∠| = 3.0e-17
   the 8th Walsh pattern is the null space               max |output| = 0.0
   mixerForward matches τ = Σ(F xᵀ − x Fᵀ) exactly       max Δ = 4.2e-17

E. torque-free motion conserves both so(4) Casimirs
   space-frame R·L·Rᵀ conserved to 2nd order in dt       1.2e-7 → 3.0e-8 (ratio 0.250)
   Casimir ‖L‖² conserved                                Δ = 7.8e-15
   Casimir Pf(L) conserved — no 3D analogue              Δ = 1.0e-18
   rotational kinetic energy conserved                   Δ = 4.3e-13

H. a hand-written pilot flies real 4D missions
   the autopilot reaches 4D waypoints                    mean 18.33 waypoints, 6 seeds
   every arrival had the w coordinate matched too        max |Δw| at arrival = 1.42 m
```

Two of these caught real bugs during development:

**The rotational integrator was losing 8.6% of ‖L‖² every 30 seconds.** Explicit
Euler does not preserve the Casimirs that the exact flow does. The drift halved
cleanly with `dt`, which identified it as truncation error rather than an
algebra mistake; RK4 on the momentum step put the same 30 s drift below 1e-11.
A drone whose angular momentum quietly decays is easier to fly than the one
being advertised.

**And then the convergence test said first order, not second.** With the
momentum step exact, the space-frame error still fell only as `dt` — because the
orientation was being advanced on the *end-of-step* angular velocity instead of
the midpoint, which is a first-order treatment of the coupling no matter how
good either half is. Trapezoidal costs one add per plane and restored second
order: the error dropped 850×, from 1.03e-4 to 1.21e-7, at an exact 0.250 ratio.
A fixed tolerance would have accepted the wrong integrator; asserting the
convergence *order* is what caught it.

## The controller

**Inputs (63 channels → 216 net inputs after a temporal window)**

| channels | what |
|---|---|
| 0–23 | 24 rangefinders fixed in the body frame: the 8 axis directions and the 16 long diagonals |
| 24–29 | gyro — the angular velocity **bivector**, one rate per body plane |
| 30–33 | accelerometer — body-frame specific force (no gravity term) |
| 34–38 | unit 4-vector to the waypoint, and the distance |
| 39–46 | how fast each of the 8 rotors is actually spinning, after motor lag |
| 47–50 | body-frame world-up — "which way is down", from the AHRS |
| 51–54 | body-frame velocity |
| 55–62 | a 4D compass: world +x and world +w in body coordinates |

Gravity pins the thrust axis but says nothing about rotation *within* the
3-space orthogonal to it — three whole degrees of freedom, the 4D generalisation
of yaw. Two more world directions close that gap; the fourth is then determined,
so there is no point spending channels on it.

Every channel is fed as several past control ticks (lags 0,1,2,3,4,6,8,10) so
the network reads rates and trends with no recurrence. **8 outputs**: rotor
throttles, 0..1. Net is `216 → 48 → 32 → 8`, 12,248 parameters, tanh hidden,
sigmoid out. Physics 120 Hz, control 30 Hz. There is no attitude controller
underneath — nothing about "hover", "level" or "up" is scripted.

## What was measured the hard way

**A hand-written pilot first, always.** When a GA fails, "the task is hard",
"the reward is broken" and "the task is impossible" all look identical.
`autopilot.js` is a textbook cascaded PD loop with one substitution: where a 3D
controller needs the rotation taking the body's thrust axis onto the desired
one — an axis, via a cross product — the 4D version builds the **plane** spanned
by those two vectors and uses the bivector ω·(b aᵀ − a bᵀ) that rotates one onto
the other. Axis → plane is the entire difference. It flies 18–19 waypoints per
episode without crashing, which proves the airframe is controllable, the mixer
is right, the arrival test is satisfiable, and any remaining trouble is a search
problem.

**Mutation must be proportional to each layer's own weight scale.** The output
layer is initialised at ~0.03× the He scale so fresh drones idle gently, giving
its weights a natural size of ~0.0075, while the first hidden layer's are
~0.096. A single flat sigma of 0.09 is a sensible step for the hidden layers and
**thirteen times the entire width of the output layer** — one mutation event
saturates a rotor and the airframe flips. Measured: generation 1 had 99% of the
population leaving the ground; generation 2, built from gen 1's elites on the
identical fixed episode bank, had **12%**. It read exactly like a crash-penalty
or reward-shaping problem and was neither — the children were being lobotomised
on the way out of the breeding loop. With per-layer scaling and a gentler rate,
a head-to-head over 25 generations gave 100% takeoff and a climbing champion
against 78% and a frozen one.

**A brain's score must not depend on which other brains share its episode.** One
sensor-noise RNG for the whole fleet advanced once per *living* drone per tick,
so as neighbours started dying at different times an elite re-flying the
identical fixed bank scored differently from one generation to the next. Fitness
stopped being a property of the brain. Each drone now draws from its own stream
seeded identically — common random numbers — so the whole fleet is perturbed the
same way and the only difference left is the brain.

**Fresh-brain behaviour is a sweep, not a guess.** The structural argument said
4D needs a *smaller* output init than 3D: tilt torque from a random output layer
grows as √(rotor count), so eight corners is √2 more tumble than four arms, and
there are three tilt planes to stay level in rather than two. The sweep said the
argument had nothing to fix — arming is saturated everywhere below 0.05 — and
that what actually separates the settings is the **fitness spread**, which peaks
at 0.03. The spread is the selection signal; a population that all scores the
same is a generation the GA cannot rank.

| outInit | left ground | armed | mean air | mean wp | fitness spread |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.050 | 100% | 81% | 2.09 s | 0.000 | 82 |
| **0.030** | **100%** | **99%** | **3.53 s** | **0.008** | **182** |
| 0.020 | 100% | 100% | 4.37 s | 0.000 | 98 |
| 0.006 | 100% | 100% | 6.18 s | 0.000 | 107 |

**The drone must be able to see the box it is being asked to stay inside.** At
the 3D project's 14 m sensor range, fourteen of the twenty-four rangefinders
read exactly zero from the spawn point — the walls were 15 m away and simply out
of range — so 28 of the 216 net inputs were dead and the drone was blind to the
wall it was drifting into until about a second before impact. Range is now 32 m,
longer than the room's 30 m longest span, so the eight axis rangefinders always
return a real distance in all four dimensions. That is less "making it easier"
than "making it observable".

**Tally crash causes across the whole population, not just the champion.** The
run was stuck with a 100% crash rate and mean airtime frozen at 4.0 s for a
hundred generations. The champion's own flight log showed it drifting into a
side wall, which suggested a navigation problem. Tallying every death across 60
brains × 3 episodes told a completely different story: the single commonest
cause was the **floor at 28%**, and the median death came at 4.9 s with the
airframe at **55° from level**. That is an attitude-control failure — the drones
were taking off, tipping over and falling back through the arming height — and
it is the bill for having three tilt planes to stay level in instead of two.

Two candidate fixes were run head to head from an identical seed. Raising the
arming height alone made things clearly *worse*, which is the informative
result: arming is also what starts airtime accruing, so raising it moves the
first rung of the ladder out of reach and takeoff fell from 100% to 80%. More
rotational damping more than paid for that. A more docile airframe is a
legitimate design choice — real multirotors vary enormously in how self-damping
they are — and nothing stabilises the drone *for* it; the network still commands
eight rotors with no attitude loop underneath.

| configuration | best | avg | airtime | took off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| baseline | 826 | 330 | 3.8 s | 100% |
| arming height 1.2 → 2.5 m | 556 | 222 | 2.5 s | 80% |
| **arming 2.5 m + damping ×2.5** | **918** | **395** | **4.4 s** | **100%** |

Raising the arming height needs `WP_FLOOR − WP_RADIUS > TAKEOFF_H`, or a drone
can hover just under the threshold — where the floor is still a landing pad and
cannot kill it — and clip low waypoints it never had to fly to.

**Physics constants must travel to the worker threads.** Each worker loads its
own copy of `drone4d.js`, so a constant patched on the main thread is invisible
to them and the fleet is silently scored under different physics than the log
claims. Overrides ride along in the episode options.

**The mission bank has to rotate, and a fixed one cost a 435-generation
plateau.** The instinct is to fix the bank for the whole run so two generations
are comparable and the champion is not merely whoever drew the easiest chain.
That concern is real, but with six fixed missions it bought a worse problem.
Drones die around eight seconds in, so they almost never reach waypoint 2 — the
training signal is six *first legs*, and six directions are memorisable.

The symptom was an unmistakable asymmetry: at generation 435, **61% of all
deaths were against the −w wall specifically**. Random brains, run as a control,
crash symmetrically across all six lateral walls (~57 each), so the physics was
not at fault. The population had evolved an open-loop "fly roughly that way"
policy fitted to six fixed first legs, with no closed-loop use of the waypoint
sensor at all — and since it was scored on exactly those six, the strategy was
locally optimal and the run could not leave it.

Resuming that population against fresh missions measured the damage exactly:

| | training bank | fresh missions |
|---|---|---|
| best fitness | 1238 | 475 |
| waypoints/episode | 0.50 | **0.00** |
| airtime | 4.8 s | 5.2 s |

The flying ability was real and transferred; the navigation did not exist. The
bank now rotates every generation, which makes missions un-memorisable. The
price is that the log's `best` column is no longer comparable across
generations, so it is not used to pick anything — `pick_champion.js` ranks the
final population on four held-out banks, folding elitism duplicates by weight
hash, with the criterion (mean waypoints across banks, tie-broken by worst bank)
fixed before any results are seen. Training seeds live in a range disjoint from
every evaluation bank by construction: with rotation the training seeds march
upward, and a naive formula walks into the held-out seeds within ~50 generations
and silently contaminates the one measurement meant to be independent.

## Rendering four dimensions honestly

You cannot show 4D, and the dishonest options — colour-coding *w*, or drawing a
slice and hoping nobody notices it is a slice — make the drone look like it is
cheating when it is not, or succeeding when it has not.

The viewer splits ℝ⁴ into two orthogonal 3-spaces that **share** the vertical and
one horizontal axis, and draws both:

    left  → span( ê_H, ŷ, ẑ )     ê_H = cos θ·x̂ + sin θ·ŵ
    right → span( ê_K, ŷ, ẑ )     ê_K = −sin θ·x̂ + cos θ·ŵ

ê_H ⊥ ê_K, so between them the two pictures carry all four coordinates with
nothing hidden and nothing double-counted. At θ = 0 the left view is the
ordinary room and the right view looks along the fourth direction. The drone is
at the waypoint only when it is on target in **both** — which is exactly the
4-ball condition, and reads immediately to someone who has never thought about
4D. θ is a user control and a real rotation of the projection basis, not a
camera trick; the room stays a box under it exactly, because projecting the
hyperrectangle onto ê_H gives ±(HX·|cos θ| + HW·|sin θ|).

**The airframe is drawn from its own geometry, not sketched.** Each propeller
disc is the exact projection of that rotor's real spin plane, built from the two
orthonormal body vectors `ROTORS[i].discA/discB` that span it — `test_math.js`
asserts `discA ∧ discB = ±S` and that both lie perpendicular to the thrust axis,
so the discs on screen are the discs in the physics. The fuselage is a unit
sphere carried by a matrix made of the *projected* body axes without
normalising, so the hull squashes exactly as an orthographic shadow of the real
4D body would when it turns through the direction that view drops. Rotor offsets
are magnified 9× because a 0.4 m airframe in a 30 m room draws as one pixel;
positions are the true ones.

A pleasing consequence: in the `x·y·z` view, rotor pairs that differ only in `w`
project on top of each other, so it looks like an ordinary quadcopter — until
the drone rotates through the fourth dimension and the props visibly separate.

**Either pilot can take over mid-flight.** The dropdown (or keys `1` and `2`)
hands the aircraft between *Mikkels AI* and the PD reference without restarting
the episode, so you can watch the same flight continue under a different
controller. The network's temporal input window survives the handover because
`Drone.control()` always runs its full sensor block and only skips the forward
pass — so the lag history is warm the moment the brain takes back over, rather
than being fed eight ticks of zeros. `space` pauses, `n` draws a new mission.

Two touches carry the rest:

- **The waypoint is drawn twice per view.** A dim wireframe sphere sits at the
  target with the full arrival radius. A bright solid sphere shows the exact
  *cross-section* of the 4-ball at the drone's own coordinate in the missing
  direction — radius √(R² − Δ²), which vanishes when the drone is far away in
  the invisible axis and reaches full size only when aligned. "I am inside the
  dim sphere but the bright one is tiny" is a picture of exactly the mistake a
  3D-thinking pilot would make.
- **Each rotor is drawn at the projection of its true 4D position**, so as the
  drone rotates in a plane you cannot see, its silhouette visibly contracts and
  expands — the way a 3D object's shadow shortens as it turns. Rotors are tinted
  by their coordinate along the *other* view's axis.

Rotor offsets from the hub are magnified 9× because a 0.4 m airframe in a 30 m
room draws as one pixel. Positions are the true ones; only the model is scaled.
