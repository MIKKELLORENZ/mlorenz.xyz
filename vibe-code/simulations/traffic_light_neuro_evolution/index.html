How it works

Every genome here is the complete set of weights and biases of one traffic-signal controller — 8 114 numbers, never touched by a gradient. The only things that change them are crossover and Gaussian mutation, and the only thing that decides which ones get copied is how many people got where they were going.

One brain runs the whole city

The genome is not one junction's timing plan. It is a single controller applied at every junction, the way a convolution applies one filter to every pixel — so the weights get feedback from all four arms of every light on every decision, and a brain evolved on a three-by-three grid can be dropped straight onto a five-by-five one it has never seen. Each light takes one decision per simulated second.

What a light can see

Its own position and which way it is currently running. Its own detectors: how many vehicles are queued on each of its four arms, how far away the nearest one is, how many have gone through lately, how many people are waiting at each crossing, how many are in a crossing right now and how long they still need, and whether the road it would release traffic onto is already full.

Then the network. Every light is on the same wire, so it also sees what its four road-connected neighbours are showing now and at twelve points in the last thirty seconds — 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 13, 15, 18, 20, 25 and 30 seconds ago — along with their identity, their distance, and how much traffic they are currently sending down the street towards it. Four more distant lights are visible at lower resolution, and a network-wide summary (what fraction of the city is running north-south, east-west, or all-pedestrian) carries the same thirty-second history. Thirty seconds is roughly how long it takes a platoon to travel one block, which is exactly the information a green wave needs.

Finally, each light gets a picture of the city: a 64×64 black-and-white road map, pooled to 16×16 for the whole plan plus a 24×24 crop centred on itself. Both go through small evolved convolutions. They are the same every second of an episode, so they are encoded once and cost nothing thereafter.

What it decides

Four choices — hold, run north-south, run east-west, or stop everything and let people cross — plus two continuous numbers: how long the amber should be, and how long the all-red after it. Minimum green, maximum green and the clearance limits are hardware, and the brain cannot violate them. Everything inside that envelope is its problem.

Where the accidents come from

Nothing about a crash is scripted. Drivers here follow the Intelligent Driver Model, take the shortest route, and obey the law. What the law says is that you stop for an amber unless you cannot do so safely — so an amber that is too short leaves cars in the dilemma zone, and they go through. If the all-red after it is also too short, the next phase is released while they are still in the box, and the two paths cross. A left-turner waiting in the middle of the junction has to be out before the cross street moves. Somebody halfway across a crossing when the traffic is released has to be given time. Every one of those is a real failure mode of a real signal, and every one of them is a consequence of a number this network chose. Long clearances are safe and throw away several seconds of capacity per cycle, per junction. There is no setting that is good at both.

How it is scored

Completed journeys are the point: +100 per vehicle and +70 per pedestrian that reaches its destination. Against that: 0.55 per second of delay for every traveller, measured against the time the same trip would have taken with every light green — including the people still stuck in traffic when the episode ends, so a controller cannot score by never letting anyone finish. A collision costs 1 400, about fourteen completed journeys, and leaves two wrecked vehicles blocking the junction for twenty-five seconds. Striking a pedestrian costs 4 200.

Common random numbers

Within a generation every genome gets the same city, the same schedule of trips, and the same drivers. Without that, a two-episode score measures the luck of the draw as much as the controller — it was the single biggest improvement in the chess sim on this site, and it is why the baseline lines on the chart mean something: those three controllers drove the identical traffic, this generation, on this map.

The streets are not a grid of straight lines

Junction boxes stay square — the phases, the crossings and the stop lines all depend on that — but the junctions themselves are nudged off the lattice and every street is a curve that leaves each junction along its arm and bends across the middle. A bend has to be driven slower than a straight, because nobody pulls more than about 3 m/s² sideways, so a winding street genuinely costs capacity.

Layouts also carry roundabouts — junctions with no signals at all, where everything gives way to whatever is already circulating and a car may only join if nothing coming round will reach the give-way line within about three seconds. The controller cannot touch those, so it has to cope with a neighbour it is unable to command. They are quick for vehicles (a quarter of the island beats waiting out a red) and hard on pedestrians, whose crossing there is much longer than at a signal. There are one-way pairs, streets that are simply missing so a four-way becomes a T, and on one tier a ring road at 80 km/h looping the whole city and joining it at every perimeter stub. Because routes are chosen by travel time, the ring changes what the shortest path even is: crossing town can be quicker the long way round.

Every generated city is closed-looped: no internal street is the only way in or out of anywhere, so nothing is a dead end and every block can be driven around. A street that would break that when removed is put back.

The curriculum

Eight cities, each harder than the last: one crossroads, then two-by-two, then nine blocks with an arterial, then winding streets with heavy foot traffic, roundabouts and a rush-hour surge, missing streets and one-way pairs, a ring road, and finally a five-by-five downtown with all of it at once where vehicles break down mid-episode and block a street. A tier unlocks when the population median matches the vehicle-actuated controller for three generations running — not when one lucky genome does. Three different street layouts are rotated within each tier so nothing can be memorised.

What it is not

This is a microsimulation, not a digital twin. Approaches have one lane, so there is no left-turn pocket and a car waiting to turn left really does hold up everyone behind it — which is why the trip table is through-dominated, as real urban grids are. Vehicles do not change route when they hit a queue, so the ring road is chosen on free-flow times rather than because it happens to be clear. Nobody jumps a red on purpose, nobody blocks the box on purpose, and there is no weather, no bus, and no cyclist.
